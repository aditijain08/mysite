Goal: land Individual Contributor (IC) roles at AI-native companies

This is a strategy framework, not a finished script. Bracketed text like [Project Name] or [metric] marks where

1. Positioning
The core problem this site has to solve
Most portfolios in the "AI-native designer" space right now skew junior — students and early-career designers
showing off flashy AI demos with no track record behind them. Most portfolios from designers with 10+ years
of experience skew the other way — quiet, writing-forward, credential-heavy, with little proof of hands-on AI
fluency. You sit in the gap between the two: real seniority, team leadership experience, and hands-on AI-native
building. That gap is your entire pitch.
Positioning statement
A Lead Product Designer who designs and builds AI-native products — someone who ships working
prototypes with AI tools as fluently as she ships Figma files, with 10+ years of craft and the judgment of
someone who has run a team, without needing to run one now.
Keep this internal — it's the sentence every page should be consistent with, not necessarily copy that appears
verbatim on the site.
Your point of view — the thesis the whole site should argue
A positioning statement says what you are. A point of view says what you believe, and that's what actually
builds empathy with someone reading the site. Yours, based on what you've described:
Design is facilitation, not authorship. The best solution can come from anywhere — a user, an engineer, a
stakeholder, a model — and my job is to create the conditions where it surfaces fast, then make it real.
This is a real, well-established design philosophy — it's the founding premise of the Google Ventures Design
Sprint: instead of one designer generating the answer alone, you facilitate a team through structured divergent
and convergent thinking (map, sketch, decide, prototype, test) to find the strongest idea in days, regardless of
whose head it came from. Naming that lineage gives your belief credibility instead of it reading as a personal
opinion.
The second half of the thesis is what makes it yours and current: sprints have always compressed the finding of
an idea, but historically stalled at a paper prototype — something you had to describe and imagine working. AI
and code remove that stall. The same facilitation instinct that ran a sprint now runs straight through to a
clickable, real interface, in roughly the same compressed timeframe. That's the throughline that connects your
philosophy of facilitation to your AI-native craft — the tools didn't change what you believe about design, they
closed the gap between the idea and the real thing.
Use this thesis as connective tissue across Home, Process, and About — it's the reason the facilitation
philosophy, the sprint background, the AI fluency, and the creative practice all belong on the same site instead
of feeling like three unrelated bios.
Two more beliefs worth stating explicitly
You raised these separately, and both are strong enough to be part of the core POV rather than buried in a case
study:
We're not feature machines. Today's designers are product builders — the job isn't just shipping what's
asked for, it's deciding what's worth building, then staying accountable for whether it actually got
adopted. That's traditionally been a PM's job to care about. It's mine too now.
Creativity isn't just visual. It's in the solution you choose, the way you think through a problem, the system
you design underneath the screens — the pixels are the smallest part of it.
The first belief is what separates "I designed a redesign" from "I designed a redesign, watched activation for
six weeks, and changed the onboarding copy when it didn't move." The second is what ties your creative
practice (painting, photography, music) back to your design craft honestly — not as a forced metaphor, but as
the same instinct applied to different mediums. Both belong in the POV section alongside the facilitation
thesis.
Four pillars (every section of the site should reinforce at least one)
1. Design craft — traditional rigor, systems thinking, and creativity applied to problems and solutions, not
just visuals.
2. Facilitation, accelerated by AI — the belief that the best answer can come from anywhere, sharpened by
sprint-style methods, now compressed further because AI and code let you visualize and ship the solution
instead of stopping at a prototype description.
3. Leadership-tested judgment — surfaced through scope and influence inside case studies ("partnered
with," "set direction for," "mentored"), never as a standalone "Leadership" section or a title. You're not
selling management — you're selling judgment that happens to have been forged by managing.
4. The human dimension — your creative practice (painting, photography, music) and your personal voice.
This is what turns "competent designer" into "person I'd want to work with," and it's the pillar most
portfolios skip entirely.
Voice and language rules
• Lead with verbs that signal IC craft: designed, built, shipped, prototyped, drove, partnered with
engineering.
• Avoid verbs and nouns that signal you're angling for management: led (as an identity, not a project verb),
managed, oversaw, Head of, Director, team of [X] reports.
• Where past team leadership comes up, frame it as evidence of judgment ("context for why my design
decisions hold up at scale"), not as a career direction.
• No AI hype language: "revolutionize," "AI-powered future," "unlock." Evidence beats adjectives — show
the prototype, the before/after, the number.
• Write in first person, active voice, short sentences. This is a craft portfolio, not a thought-leadership blog.
• Voice can warm up as the visitor goes deeper. Home and Work stay crisp and evidence-led — that's what
gets you taken seriously in the first 90 seconds. Process and About can be warmer and more personal —
that's what makes someone feel like they know you, which is where empathy actually gets built.

within those five, Process and About now carry more weight than a purely resume-optimized site would give
them, because the point of view and the human side are core to what you want this site to do.

3. Page-by-page content
Home
Headline (sample options — pick one, don't use all)
• "Lead Product Designer. I design AI-native products and build the prototypes myself."
• "Product design, built with AI. 10+ years of craft, currently obsessed with what's possible when design
and code collapse into one loop."
• "10+ years designing products. Now I ship with AI as fast as I used to sketch."
Subhead
One line stating current focus + what makes you different: e.g. "Currently designing [type of product] at
[Company]. I use Cursor and Claude Code to prototype my own ideas end to end, not just hand off specs."
Point-of-view line (optional but recommended)
One short sentence bridging to the Process page, so the facilitation thesis is present from the first screen, not
buried on page three: e.g. "I believe the best solution can come from anywhere — my job is to get to it fast,
then make it real." Link it to Process. This is what starts building empathy before someone even reaches
About.
Experience strip (borrowed from Rupa Chaturvedi's site — a must-have)
Rupa's site breaks her years of experience into named dimensions instead of one flat "10+ years" — Player /
Coach / Specialist / Innovator / Educator, each with its own year count. It works because it shows range in one
glance instead of forcing someone to read a bio to find it. Adapt the same structure to your own
facilitator/builder identity rather than copying her labels — something like:
• BUILDER — [X] yrs — Product Design
• FACILITATOR — [X] yrs — Running sprints, workshops, cross-team alignment
• LEADER — [X] yrs — Set direction for small design teams
• AI-NATIVE — [X] yrs or "since 2023/2024" — Designing and building with AI tools
Four rows is enough. Resist adding a fifth just to match Rupa's five — hers earned five because each one is
genuinely distinct in her career; yours should only have as many as are true.
Proof strip
A slim row under the fold: companies/logos you've worked with, and one differentiator stat if you have one
(e.g. "Shipped to X users," "Reduced onboarding drop-off by X%"). This is separate from the experience strip
above — don't duplicate the years count here.
Featured work preview
2–3 case study cards with a one-line outcome each — not a project title alone. E.g. "Redesigned [flow] — cut
task completion time from 4 steps to 1."
CTA
Single, clear next action: "See the full case study" or "View my work." Avoid multiple competing CTAs on
the home page.
Work / Case Studies
3–4 projects, each shown as a native thumbnail card on the site (title, one-line outcome, cover image) that
opens a Notion deep-dive on click — see Section 6 for how to make that handoff feel intentional. The
thumbnail itself still needs to do real work: don't just show a project name, show the one-line outcome, since
some visitors will scan thumbnails without clicking through. Each Notion deep-dive follows the AI-native case
study template in Section 4. Prioritize depth over quantity — one excellent deep-dive beats five thin ones. At
least one case study should explicitly involve AI product design (designing an AI feature/experience), and at
least one should show you using AI tools in your own process, even if the product itself isn't AI-related.
Process (formerly "Lab" — now the philosophy + build page)
This page carries the thesis from Section 1. It has two halves, in this order:
Half one — the belief
• Open with the facilitation statement in your own words: design isn't about being the sole author of the
answer — it's about creating the conditions for the right answer to surface, from wherever it comes.
• Name the sprint lineage briefly — e.g. "I learned this running Google-style Design Sprints: map the
problem, diverge fast, converge on one bet, prototype it, test it — all in days, not months." One or two
sentences, not a sprint tutorial.
• Make the AI bridge explicit: "What's changed is what a sprint can end in. It used to end in a paper
prototype and a story you told about how it would work. Now it can end in something real — a working
interface you can click, break, and ship." This is the sentence that ties your philosophy to your craft.
• Say plainly that speed isn't the same as quality: "AI makes prototypes fast. Good design still takes time —
the prototype is where thinking starts, not where it ends." This protects you from reading as someone who
thinks AI shortcuts craft, which is the biggest risk of leading with an AI-native pitch.
Optional: an interactive point-of-view map (good-to-have, high payoff if done well)
Rupa Chaturvedi's site has a section called "POV" — a dark, clickable node map of short belief-statements
("Delegation, not automation," "Trust as architecture," "Taste as differentiator"), grouped into two or three
clusters, that a visitor drags around and clicks into to read a paragraph each. It's a genuinely distinctive way to
present a philosophy instead of a wall of text, and it doubles as proof you can build something interactive. If
you attempt this, it belongs on the Process page as an alternate way to present the beliefs already listed in
Section 1 — the facilitation thesis, the "not a feature machine" line, and the creativity-is-not-just-visual line all
work well as nodes. Don't attempt this unless you're confident you (or an AI-assisted build) can execute it
cleanly — a half-built version of this pattern looks worse than not having it, since the whole point is that it's a
craft flex.
Before you build / after you ship — a rigor checklist
This directly answers your point that speed isn't quality, and that outcomes are a designer's job too, not just a
PM's. Structure each case study (or the Process page generally) around two short checklists framed around a
real build:
Before the prototype:
• Who is this for — real users in a real moment, not a persona of convenience.
• What problem matters — the job to be done, weighed against everything else it could have been.
• What behavior changes — name the shift you expect to see, before you build, not after.
• What you're deliberately not building — scope cuts are design decisions, not compromises.
After the prototype — the monitor-and-improve phase you own, not just PM:
• Validation — real users, real tasks, evidence decides what actually ships.
• Edge cases and states — empty, loading, error, extreme; the happy path is the easy path.
• Systems thinking — how it fits the rest of the product: patterns, components, consistency.
• Content — words are the interface; placeholder copy isn't finished design.
• Accessibility — if it doesn't work for everyone, it doesn't work.
• Failure and refinement — recovery paths and the polish pass that make it shippable, plus what you'd
change now that it's live.
Using even a compressed version of this (2–3 "before" bullets, 2–3 "after" bullets) inside each case study
makes the "outcomes matter to designers too" point through structure, not just a claim in a paragraph.
Working with engineering (good-to-have, strong signal for AI-native teams)
A short paragraph or bullet list on how you actually work day to day with engineers now — this is different
from "I used AI to prototype" and worth calling out separately:
• Raising PRs yourself for smaller tickets — copy tweaks, spacing, states — instead of only handing off
specs.
• Doing design QA directly against staging/shipped builds, not just reviewing static Figma.
• Giving feedback in the same tools engineers use (GitHub comments, Linear, etc.) rather than only in
Figma.
This is a small section — three to five lines — but it's exactly the kind of detail that separates "I've heard of
AI-native workflows" from "I actually work this way," and most senior portfolios don't have it at all.
Half two — the proof
This is where the old "Lab" content lives, reframed as evidence of the belief above rather than a standalone
tools showcase:
• What you built (a real, working thing — a tool, a prototype, a redesign of something real).
• The stack ("Cursor + Claude Code + [framework]") — signals you're fluent with the actual tools AI-native
teams use.
• Why you built it — tie it back to a real facilitation moment: a workshop, a stuck team, a debate that a
working prototype settled faster than a slide deck would have.
• A link or embedded demo if it's live. If it's not live, a short screen recording beats static screenshots.
Optionally include a short "how I run a session" visual — a simple before/after or timeline graphic showing
sprint-style steps compressing into an AI-assisted build. This page can be more visually loose than Work —
that's appropriate for a page about process.
About
Keep the professional portion to 150–250 words, then let the creative section breathe — this page is where the
site stops being a resume and starts being a person, which is exactly what you said you want it to do. Structure:
5. Where you are now and what you focus on (1–2 sentences).
6. Your trajectory — how you got to senior craft + AI fluency (2–3 sentences).
7. The leadership line — one sentence, past tense, framed as experience not identity: e.g. "Along the way
I've set direction and mentored designers on small teams — experience that shapes how I think about
scale, even though my focus now is hands-on craft."
8. The creative practice — its own short paragraph, not a throwaway line: you paint sneakers, shoot
photography, play guitar and sing, and generally experiment across mediums. Don't apologize for it or
over-explain why it's relevant — the connection to your design sensibility (an eye for detail, comfort
iterating in public, ease moving between mediums) can be one honest sentence, not a forced metaphor
stretched across a paragraph.
If you have decent photos of the sneaker painting, photography work, or a short clip of you playing/singing, a
small visual strip here does more for empathy than any amount of copy — people connect to seeing the work,
not just reading that it exists.
Contact / Resume
Email, LinkedIn, resume PDF download. Nothing else. Do not bury this behind a form if you can help it —
recruiters bounce off contact forms.
Put the resume link in the global nav or header too (e.g. "Resume" as its own nav item, or a persistent
download icon), not only on the Contact page. A recruiter skimming Home shouldn't have to hunt for it — this
is a one-line build note but an easy thing to forget.
4. Case Study Template — AI-Native Framing
This is the structure that separates a portfolio that merely mentions AI from one that proves AI-native
judgment. Use it for every case study, even ones where the product itself isn't AI-powered — sections 3 and 6
can be adapted to "how I used AI in my own process" if the product doesn't warrant them.
1. Snapshot
Role, timeline, company/context, team size, one-line outcome. Three to five lines max — this is the scan-infive-seconds section.
2. The problem, and the capability gap
Frame the challenge not just as a user problem but as a gap between what the underlying technology CAN do
and what the experience SHOULD do. E.g.: "The model could generate a full rewrite instantly. But users
needed targeted, reviewable edits — not a wall of new text to re-read." This is the single highest-signal move
in an AI-native case study; it shows systems thinking, not just visual design.
3. Process — human + AI collaboration
Show, don't claim. Include: how you used AI tools in the design process itself (prototyping in Cursor/Claude
Code, testing prompt behavior, live-wiring a model to validate an interaction before it was buildable).
Screenshots of actual iterations beat a paragraph describing your process.
4. Craft decisions
Standard UX rigor: research, flows, systems, edge cases, states (loading, error, timeout, low-confidence
output). This is where your traditional design depth carries the weight — don't let the AI framing crowd it out.
5. The business case
Where possible, connect the design decision to cost, latency, or conversion — not just usability. E.g. "Reduced
average model calls per session by [X], directly lowering inference cost" or "Cut time-to-first-response from
[X]s to [X]s, which correlated with a [X]% drop in early abandonment." If you don't have hard metrics, use
qualitative proof honestly: shipped status, stakeholder feedback, user quotes — don't manufacture numbers.
6. Trust, control, and the human-in-the-loop
Show how the interface lets users correct, override, or steer the AI — and if relevant, how you tested for
failure modes or bias in the output. This section is rare in most portfolios and reads as maturity when it's there.
7. Outcome and reflection
What shipped, what you'd do differently, what you learned. One honest limitation or tradeoff is more credible
than a clean win narrative.
5. What to gather before writing
Since we built this framework before your specific project details, here's what to pull together next:
• 3–5 candidate projects — pick the ones with the clearest capability-gap story and the most concrete
outcome, not necessarily the most visually polished.
• Any metrics tied to each project, even directional ones (adoption, retention, cost, time saved).
• 1–2 self-directed builds for the Process page — if you don't have one yet, this is worth spending a
weekend on before launch; it's the single highest-leverage page for your specific positioning.
• A resume PDF formatted for the Lead Product Designer / IC track — no direct-report counts, no
"managed team of X" as a headline bullet.
• Photos or clips of your creative work — sneaker paintings, photography, guitar/vocal performance — even
a phone photo of a painted sneaker is enough to start with; polish later.
• A specific, real memory of facilitation working — a moment a sprint, workshop, or fast prototype settled a
debate or surfaced an answer that didn't come from you. That anecdote is probably your strongest Process
page opener.
6. Content Architecture: Notion vs. Native
Project thumbnails live natively on the Work page; a click opens a Notion (or Notion-style) doc for the full
deep-dive; Home, Process, About, and the Work page shell all stay fully native on the site. This resolves the
main risk of a Notion-heavy site — the AI-native "I build things" signal doesn't depend on the case study
deep-dives, because Process already carries it (POV statement, the build-log proof, the engineeringcollaboration section). Notion is a genuinely good fit for the deep-dive layer specifically, since the case study
template in Section 4 is text-and-structure heavy (capability gap, checklist, HITL framing) and Notion handles
that kind of long-form reading well.
Two things worth doing to make the handoff feel intentional rather than like leaving the site:
• Publish the Notion doc in its clean/public view (sidebar and Notion chrome hidden), and pick a cover,
font, and accent color that roughly match your site's palette — it won't be pixel-identical, but it shouldn't
feel like a jarring context switch either.
• Open the deep-dive in a new tab from the thumbnail, so your polished site stays open in the background
and the visitor doesn't feel like they've left it behind.
Process stays fully native regardless — that page is where the interactive POV map (if you build it) and the
build-log proof live, and both depend on custom interaction Notion can't replicate.
